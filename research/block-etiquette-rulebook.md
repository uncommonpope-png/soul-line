# Block Etiquette Rulebook — Research Report

## Summary

Authentic "block etiquette" is not one list. It is a **reputation economy** enforced without writing: you learn by watching from the doorway / bottom step / folding chair, you are corrected by a look before you are corrected by words, and the penalty is rarely a fine — it is a story told about you all summer ("Auntie Beulah will be talking about you... for the rest of the summer"), loss of a seat in the rotation, loss of store credit for the whole block, or loss of an invitation that was never formally issued and can be silently revoked.

Five master principles recur in almost every source, across regions:

1. **Speak first.** Greet the room, the stoop, the elders, *before* food, business, or sitting down. Failure reads not as shyness but as rudeness / no home training.
2. **Elders and children eat / sit / speak first; the cook / grill master / host eats last and controls their station absolutely.**
3. **Labour creates a temporary claim** — shovelling a parking spot, cooking a dish well for years, holding a spades seat — but the claim expires (48 hours in Boston law; when everyone has had a first plate; when the host says so).
4. **Don't take out more than you put in** — drinks, to-go plates, borrowed sugar/tools, store credit, a plus-one seat. Contribute visibly, ask first, return in better condition.
5. **What happens in the space stays in the space** — barbershop talk, family business, a neighbour's business you happened to see from the porch. Discretion *is* the etiquette; the porch / candy lady / barber *see* everything precisely so they don't have to *say* everything.

Confidence: **High for cookout, spades, dibs, stoop, elder-greeting, streetlight, barbershop, grill** — multiple independent documented lists agree verbatim. **Medium for corner store, dominoes, rent party (historical), candy lady** — strong single traditions + corroborating social posts. **Lower / more generic for laundromat, bus, moving day** — sources found were largely general U.S. etiquette, not specifically Black urban sources; flagged as such below. Do not present those three as culture-specific.

How to use this for a scoring satire series: the comedy engine everywhere in the sources is **mock-legal codification of a tiny act** — "Thou shall not make a to-go plate before everybody made their first plate," "Man Law: Chapter 6" for eye contact in the barber chair, a grill rule requiring "two forms of government ID, a blood sample, and DNA." Score the *violation*, not the identity. See §17 and Stereotype Risk.

## Findings

### 1. Stoop / Porch Rules

| Rule | Phrase people use | Violation | Social meaning / cost | Comedy angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The stoop belongs to who lives there; guests get a grace period | "Permission Slope" — if you don't live there "you have about a 15-minute grace period before it gets weird, unless you're actively talking to someone who *does* live there" | Parking yourself on a stranger's steps, settling in | You're claiming a living room you weren't invited into | A timer visibly running on a tourist sitting down |
| Bottom steps = public, top steps = inner sanctum | "The Bottom Step Rule" — tying a shoe / waiting for a ride: "stay on the bottom two steps. The top steps are the 'inner sanctum'" | Climbing to the top step uninvited | You've skipped three levels of relationship in one climb | Treat steps like airline boarding groups |
| Acknowledge everyone who passes | "Head Nod Economy" — "If you're sitting on a stoop and a neighbor walks by, you acknowledge them. This isn't Midtown. You aren't anonymous" | Staring at your phone, no greeting | You are declaring the block is not a community — you are a visitor | See also The Nod, §10 |
| Leave nothing behind | "Trash is Sin: If you leave a coffee cup on someone's stoop, you are a villain. End of story" | Litter on steps | Lowest-effort disrespect; everyone knows whose cup it was | Villain origin story = one coffee cup |
| Porch as neutral ground, elder's chair is reserved | Southern porch version (viral reel): "never sit in papa's chair, even if it's empty, because the owner will appear like a ghost"; "no business talk / no drama on the porch — only gossiping while shelling peas, waving at passersby"; porch swing is "for couples or naps, nothing in between" | Sitting in the empty best chair; bringing an argument to the porch | The empty chair is the *most* taken chair on the block | Owner materialises the second your weight hits the cushion |

Source: Diffsense, "Stoop and the City" — verified live. Jane Jacobs' "eyes on the street" is the academic frame the article uses: grandmothers on the top step watching kids, teenagers on the bottom step — the stoop as neighbourhood surveillance *and* safety. Porch-culture histories make the same point: a line of chairs facing the street invites passersby to add a chapter; children sit on steps quiet at first, then earn questions.

**Noise corollary (block-wide):** music / loud talk from a porch is licensed *by participation* — the block tolerates what it can join. After-hours noise from a closed-door house party with no neighbours invited is the violation. See §7.

### 2. Corner Store / Bodega Etiquette

Names are local and argued about with pride: corner store, bodega (NYC), papi store (Philly Latino-owned stores), "the store."

| Rule | Phrase | Violation | Social meaning | Comedy angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Know your order before the counter | Bodega guides: "by the time you reach the front of the line, you know what you want and you're ready to pay"; don't invent a complicated custom order at rush hour | Holding the line designing an omelette / specialty roll in a cheesesteak shop (Philly reel rule 3) | You're treating a neighbourhood institution like a private chef | Whiteboard-professor sketch literally lectures this |
| Take fights outside | Philly "papi store rules" #1: "Do not fight inside — take any dispute outside" | Arguing / fighting in the aisles | The store is neutral / safe ground — a refuge; clerks will shelter a regular who feels followed | The most serious rule delivered as Rule 1 of a comedy lecture, and commenters treat it as law |
| Respect the store cat | "Respect the store cat, which is considered family, and leave it alone even if it's sleeping on bread" | Moving / complaining about the cat | Cat outranks customers; the cat *lives* here, you don't | Sacred-animal bit that every city claims as its own discovery |
| Honour store credit — for everyone | "If the owner gives you credit, pay it back, otherwise you'll ruin it for the whole block" — commenters repeatedly call repayment "law" | Running a tab / "on the book" and disappearing | This is the sharpest one: your default doesn't just shame you, it *ends credit for neighbours who honoured it* | One person's $4 tab closes a community bank |
| The store is an information exchange, known by its owner | Philly unwritten-rules list: stores "serve as part grocery, part restaurant, and part neighborhood information exchange... better known by their owner than their official business name"; flyers for lost cats, block parties, yard sales on the door for decades | Treating it as an anonymous convenience store; photographing it as a tourist attraction without asking | You're missing that you're standing in the block's bulletin board / front desk | Regular "barely needs to explain their regular order" vs. newcomer reciting a full biography to order coffee |
| Get a bag and a receipt | From "The Black Rules" list (see §18): "Always ask for a bag and a receipt, no matter how small the purchase" + "Keep your hands visible while shopping. Don't give anyone a reason to accuse you of stealing" | Walking out holding an item openly with no bag | **Handle with care — this is a survival rule, not folksy etiquette.** It documents being watched in stores. Use only if the series can hold the serious undertow; never as a light gag alone | None recommended standalone; if used, the joke must be on the watching, not the watched |

Sources: Philadelphia papi-store reel and Philly unwritten-rules articles, NYC bodega etiquette guides, Black Sociology "Black Rules" — index.

### 3. Cookout Law — the most documented system in the research

The cookout has *two* layers, and sources keep them distinct: (a) the literal backyard event, with enforceable food law; (b) "The Cookout" as metaphor — an invite-only trust space. A book that confuses them will get both wrong.

#### 3a. Literal cookout law

| Rule | Phrase people use | Violation | Social meaning / cost | Comedy angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Speak before you eat | 92Q list, first rule: "Speak Before You Eat — You greet the family before heading to the food table"; Say It Lady: sponsor "walks you around to greet every elder in that yard" | Beelining to the foil pans | You came for food, not people — everybody clocked it | Guest GPS track goes straight past Grandma to the ribs |
| Don't bring unapproved plus-ones | Urban Girl: "Don't show up... with a plus 1,2,3, or 4 without asking first"; BBQ Etiquette reel: limit plus-ones (creator says two max) and "you cannot invite [others] to the Q" | Arriving with a carload | Host cooked / budgeted for a number; your guests eat someone's plate — and *you* are liable for everything they do (see sponsor rule) | Plus-one math escalating like a wedding |
| Never show up empty-handed — but don't bring a *dish* unless assigned | "Don't show up empty handed" (every list); "Even if they say 'just come,' bring drinks, ice, chips, or something useful"; first-timer rule: contribution is "a chance to ASK how you can contribute... In a worst case, bring ice"; outsider-guest rule (Say It Lady): "you may not cook something and bring it... bring liquor if confirmed, full-sugar soda — never that sparkling water mess — or a store-bought fruit tray... Everything has to arrive in a supermarket bag" | (i) Empty hands; (ii) a surprise homemade casserole competing with assigned dishes; (iii) sparkling water as a contribution | Empty hands = taker. Unassigned dish = you don't trust / don't know the hierarchy of cooks. Assigned dishes (mac & cheese, potato salad) are held by named people for years: "If potato salad is not your strong suit stay away from making"; "at least three people believe they make the best mac and cheese... This dish needs to be tasked to one person only"; "Potato salad (no raisin preferred)"; mac "must be baked — box mac doesn't count" | The dish-audition system: one bad potato salad is remembered longer than a good one. Raisins in potato salad is the recurring "Black Card Revoked" instant-fail answer |
| Elders / children first; cook eats last; no seconds until all have had firsts | Uncle Dibbz: "Kids usually go first, followed by our respected elders. After they've served up, it's all systems go"; Urban Girl: "Wait until the elderly and children have eaten before you indulge in seconds... don't be greedy"; Vogeg: "Don't Touch That Chicken Yet — Auntie Has First Dibs... until that one auntie gets her plate and her chair set up in the shade, you better wait"; 92Q: "The Cook Eats Last" | Loading a mountain first plate; going back while someone hasn't eaten | Greed is the cardinal cookout sin — more than bad cooking. 92Q: "The First Plate Should Be Calm — People judge overloaded first plates. Save the big plate for round two"; "Keep the Food Line Moving — Don't block the table being indecisive"; "One Dish Always Looks Suspicious — there's always one dish people wait to see someone else try first" | First-plate architecture judged like a diving score; the suspicious dish sitting untouched with a full audience |
| **To-go plate law — the single most repeated rule in all sources** | Ross Mac, "Black Barbecue Commandments": **"Thou shall not make a to-go plate before everybody made their first plate"**; Urban Girl: "Do not pack a 'to go' plate until you are actually ready to go... It's very rude to start hoarding food as soon as you show up"; Jamaican cookout list (diaspora parallel): "Do not pack a to-go plate unless the host has asked you to. Also, do not travel with Tupperware. If you must, do not take it out your car... hang onto the foil covered paper plate... then do a quick swap in the privacy of your car" | Foil out on arrival; stacking multiple to-go plates at 6pm before late guests arrive; arriving *with* containers | You are treating hospitality as takeaway and taking a second meal out of a first-meal pot. Community debate threads land on: host announces when plates may be packed; one plate per person; legitimate early-leavers (ill, elderly) excepted. A confessional comedy reel doing it *on arrival* drew 690 comments, polarised — proof the rule has heat | Mock-Ten-Commandments framing is already the native format. Foil as a tell: "we heard the foil before we saw you." The car-Tupperware swap as a covert operation |
| BYOB means BYOB | "If you plan to enjoy an alcoholic beverage bring something to contribute to the bar. No one likes the person who never brings alcohol but stands by the bar all night"; Steve Harvey Bobby-Q rule: "Don't drink more than you brought"; cooler rule (BBQ reel): don't help yourself to the cooler without invitation — kids' drinks are separate from alcohol | Drinking up the bar / raiding the cooler | Visible mooching; your bottle should be *seen* being contributed ("bring a bottle and make it visible") | Cooler as a bank vault with a guard; kids'-juice-box theft as the lowest denomination of crime |
| Don't wander the house | Cookout-rules reel: stay in "backyard, living room, and kitchen and ask for the bathroom rather than wandering the house" | Touring bedrooms | The invite is to the *function*, not the house | Guest found reviewing a medicine cabinet like a museum |
| The best meat gets hidden — accept it | 92Q: "The Best Meat Gets Hidden — Somebody always saves a private tray of ribs or hot links for later"; cook/host's prerogative | Demanding the hidden tray; exposing it | The cook's tax / family reserve is legitimate and unmentionable | Everyone knows about the tray. Nobody has seen the tray. The tray is Schrödinger's ribs |
| Don't hijack the music | Uncle Dibbz: "Don't be trying to hijack the aux cord"; Bobby-Q: "Don't mess with the music... If it's not old-school R&B or soul, it's not getting any airtime" at Dad's | Seizing aux / Bluetooth | DJ / Dad controls a station like the grill master controls the pit (see §3b) | Aux cord as a second set of tongs |
| Clean-up and stay-duration | Reel comment consensus + lists: help clean, don't overstay; first-timer guidance: arrive 90 min–2 hrs after stated time (host time is not guest time), leave "before you've outstayed your welcome" | Arriving on the dot to an empty yard and a host still seasoning; camping past the chairs being folded | Timing *is* etiquette; the stated time is information, not an instruction, and insiders know the offset | **Caution:** arrival-offset varies by family/region — play it as a house rule to ask your sponsor about, not a universal law (see Stereotype Risk) |

#### 3b. The grill master's authority

Unanimous, cross-source, and the best-documented single role:

- "Don't Touch the Grill. The grill belongs to the grill master only. If you weren't asked to help, stay away from it. This goes for in the kitchen with the sides as well." — 92Q / Rickey Smiley lists, verified live.
- "Respect the Grill Master... Don't be that guy giving unsolicited advice or worse, trying to take over the grill without an invitation — that's a major no-no!" — Uncle Dibbz, verified live.
- Steve Harvey Bobby-Q Rule #1 via The Soul Food Pot: "Don't touch Dad's grill… Unless you want to 'draw back a nub!'... This is sacred ground." — index.
- Vogeg: "Don't hover. Don't offer advice. Don't question his timing. He is the chosen one — tongs in hand, sweat on his forehead, towel over his shoulder. He runs the cookout. Period." AURN: "Every Black family has *that* uncle or cousin who's king of the grill. Give him his tongs, his towel over the shoulder, and don't question his technique." Also: elders' seating, spades/dominoes table, and food table are separate stations — "Don't mix them up. Respect the hierarchy."
- Hyperbole version (comedy-native): "never touch another man's grill unless you have pre-authorization, two forms of government ID, a blood sample, and DNA... I don't care if the grill is on fire" — the joke works because the underlying rule needs no exaggeration to be absolute.
- **The designated chair corollary:** "Don't You Dare Sit in *That* Chair... old folding chair with duct tape and history. It's been Uncle Ricky's throne since 1997." (Vogeg)

#### 3c. "Invited to the Cookout" — the metaphor

- Origin documented by Know Your Meme / Say It Lady: hashtag **#WhitePeopleInvitedToTheCookout, Feb. 29, 2016** (Know Your Meme page carries a 2019 typo in one line — its own correction and Say It Lady both give 2016), founding list: Leonardo DiCaprio, Betty White, Justin Timberlake, Paul Wall, Steve Nash.
- Meaning: an outsider is *trusted* in Black spaces, not merely tolerated. Criteria are unwritten "which is exactly why they work. You cannot buy your way onto the list with a single well-timed Instagram post... You cannot earn it by using AAVE correctly one time." Pattern over time, standing somewhere uncomfortable — graded by self-appointed judges: "a more rigorous peer review process than most published journals run."
- **Limits that are part of the rule, not footnotes:** the invite does **not** include using the N-word or putting on AAVE for the weekend; it is **one-time / revocable** ("Any invite to The Cookout is a ONE TIME INVITE. There are no season passes"); Black people themselves can be *disinvited* for conduct against the community; there is no unanimous list — "One person's invitation is another person's rejected application, and we're both allowed to be right."
- **Sponsor liability:** your inviter answers for you — check what to bring with them "so they don't catch a spades-level beat down for bringing you in the first place." This sponsor principle generalises: at any block function, who brought you is your credit rating.
- Druski's "The Cookout Approved White Boy" / "WhiteBoy accepted by the Hood" sketches (30.7M views listed) are the current mass version of exactly this bit — a character folded into the circle — and his later whiteface work sparked the debate in §17 about where recognition satire tips over.

### 4. Barbershop and Salon Etiquette

**Barbershop** (DreBladez list, verified live, + reels, index):

| Rule | Phrase | Violation / cost |
|---|---|---|
| Speak to the room on entry | "Morning, gentlemen" — two words, then nod to the barber first, then to the oldest man present (Detroit 1964 lesson retold in viral reel); "by the time your name was called... you were no stranger"; don't take the best chair if an older man is standing | Walking in silent and sitting = you announced you have no home training to a room built to notice |
| Don't sit in the chair unless invited | "Don't sit in the barber's chair unless invited. Period. No spinning. No leaning. It's not a carnival ride" | The chair is a workstation/throne, not waiting-room furniture |
| Don't rush, don't back-seat cut | "Don't rush your barber. Great cuts take time... if you picked them, let them do what they do"; don't ask "who's the sharpest" in the shop — it forces a ranking in front of everyone; ask about hair *type* experience, not race | Insulting the craft in its own house |
| Loyalty to your barber is real; cheating is a social event | Waiting for *your* barber while another chair is free is normal; sitting in another barber's chair in view of yours is read as defection (the "reckless eyeballing" bit — below — grew from chair protocol) | You will be discussed |
| Appointment truthfulness | "Respect appointment times. If you're running behind, say something. Walk-ins are cool, but don't get salty if appointments get seen first"; don't try to bribe your way into someone else's slot | Lateness without a call steals from the *next* client, who is also from the neighbourhood |
| What's said in the shop stays in the shop | "What's said in the shop is for the shop... a safe zone, not social media content"; barber-side version: don't use another barber's tools without asking, don't jump into other people's conversations, clean up behind yourself, "not everything that happens in the shop is public conversation" | Recording / repeating shop talk outside = you end the shop's function as a safe space |
| Volume | "Phones on vibrate. No FaceTime in the chair. Don't be the loudest one in the room. This ain't a podcast" | Speakerphone in the chair is the modern canonical violation |
| Intergenerational protocol | "OG's, speak life. Young men, listen more than you talk. The shop is where game gets passed down" | A young client lecturing the room inverts the shop's purpose |
| Tip | "If you love the cut, say it with your wallet. Especially if your barber squeezed you in" | A squeeze-in repaid with exact change is remembered at the next squeeze-in request |
| Eye contact during a line-up | Viral "Unwritten Rules: Barber Ethics" bit: "reckless eyeballing" — staring into your barber's eyes while he cuts; clients close their eyes during the line-up; commenters codify it as "Man Law: Chapter 6" | Pure mock-law comedy built on a real discomfort — a perfect template for the series: a tiny physical awkwardness + a fake statute number |

**Salon** (index; stylist-side sources): the mirror image is *stylist time is inventory*.
- "Book your appointment, show up on time, and pay your deposit. It's that simple... stop playing with these stylists' time."
- Written policies that grew from unwritten ones: non-refundable deposit, 15 minutes late = fee / possible cancellation, 30 minutes = cancelled + deposit lost, come with hair washed / prepped as instructed, limited unserviced guests (often one, waiting area only; children only if being serviced).
- **Comedy-native backlash format:** viral rants *parodying* ever-longer booking-policy lists ("bring your own... arrive washed... I will cancel if your energy is off... don't text me 'hey boo'") — audiences laugh at *both* the late client and the 40-rule policy page. A scoring series should keep that two-way aim: the rule is real, the rulebook's inflation is the joke.

### 5. Parking Spot Law — "Dibs"

The best-documented *regional* system, with names: **dibs** (Chicago), **space savers** (Boston), **parking chair** (Pittsburgh), **savesies** (Philadelphia).

| Element | Documented detail |
|---|---|
| The claim | You shovel out a snow-buried car, you place a marker — lawn chair, cones, trash cans, ladders, ironing boards, a table; Boston sightings include a life-size Rob Gronkowski cutout and a Virgin Mary statue ("You would never move that") — and the spot is yours while snow remains |
| The theory | Sources explicitly call it Lockean: labour mixed with the spot creates temporary entitlement. Chicago History Museum historian Peter Alter traces the entitlement claim to the Big Snow of 1967 (23 inches); a Feb. 9, 1967 *Tribune* editorial "Post-Blizzard Etiquet[te]" already describes motorists "staking out their domains with folding chairs, carpenters' horses, and anything else that may come to hand." Term "dibs" coined in print in 1999 |
| The limits (unwritten constitution) | You must actually have shovelled (exposed pavement); you may not shovel your snow *into* someone else's spot or onto the sidewalk; no claiming before the storm ends / before a snow emergency is declared; no holding while you're simply at work in some neighbourhood formulations; the claim **expires** |
| The law vs. the block | Boston: savers *allowed* for **48 hours after a declared snow emergency ends** (since 2005), banned outright in the South End, removed by trash crews after. Chicago: dibs is **illegal** (§10-28-070) and periodically removed by Streets & Sanitation (from March 2, 2021) — and traditionally unenforced, with ~2,000 dibs complaints in one recent winter stretch. Pittsburgh: no governing rule, police call chairs technically "abandoned furniture," city sells novelty "Official Parking Chairs" — tradition protected by custom. Philly: savesies condemned by authorities, defended in South Philly. Baltimore's mayor in 2010 declined to enforce the ban: unstoppable, "like telling people they can't say 'Hon'" |
| The cost of violating | Moving a saver is "one of the more reliable ways to start a genuine dispute on a Philly block." Documented Chicago retaliation in police reports / NPR investigation of 64 dibs vandalism reports: slashed tires most common, then broken windows, keying, spray paint, mirrors — one arrest. WAMC 2026 formulation of the street rule: "If you move someone's space saver and park your car in its place, you have no right to complain about what happens to your car" |
| The counter-law | "You leave it, you lose it" — former NYT Ethicist Randy Cohen: shovelling your car out is "simply the price you pay for storing your private property in our public space." The debate is *inside* the culture, not outsiders vs. insiders — a scoring series must give the anti-dibs neighbour a real argument, because real blocks do |
| Non-snow version | Chicago reel/discussion: "do not park in front of a neighbor's house, and do not allow guests to do so" is taught as a freestanding respect rule even though the curb is public — the spot *in front of a home* is socially assigned to that home |

Sources: Wikipedia "Parking chair" (verified live, well-referenced), Chicago dibs history, WAMC/NPR 2026, Philly lists — index + verified.

### 6. Borrowing and Favors

The sources are thinner and more general than for cookouts — present these as block-general with Black-household formulations where documented, not as a codified canon.

- **Tools / dishes / ladders:** "You didn't borrow something without asking. If someone loaned you something, you were expected to return it in the same condition... If a neighbor loaned your family a tool, casserole dish, ladder, or cup of sugar, it went back... take care of things, especially when they don't belong to you." (Household-rules retrospective)
- **The dish rule (cross-cultural, widely repeated):** never return a borrowed / gifted-food dish empty — return it with food in it. In block terms: the plate that comes back empty announces the relationship is over.
- **Sugar / eggs / ingredients — the small-amount economy:** forum consensus across multiple neighbour-borrowing discussions: a cup of sugar, an egg or two, a splash of milk mid-recipe is a *gift economy* item — not literally repaid, repaid by reciprocity when the lender is mid-recipe. A whole bag of flour / dozen eggs / full carton taken repeatedly and never replaced crosses into abuse: "at what point does being neighborly become abusive?" Frequency is the violation — once or twice is neighbourhood; monthly becomes a grocery strategy, and lenders switch to "Sorry, I don't have any" on repeat, which everyone understands as a closed account.
- **Money:** no strong culture-specific documented list was found in this research (see Could Not Verify). The generalisable documented principle from the credit contexts that *are* documented — store credit (§2) and dibs labour (§5) — is: a small informal loan is a *block* resource; defaulting costs the defaulter reputation and costs everyone else access. Do not invent a money-lending canon.
- **Rides / favours:** block-party research notes the long game explicitly: by year five of a block party you'd "feel comfortable... asking any of your block party neighbors" for a ride or urgent help — i.e., favours are drawn on an account built by showing up, not requested cold. The favour rule is therefore sequenced: **relationship first, request second.** A newcomer whose first interaction is a big ask has violated the order, whatever the ask is.
- **Comedy angle:** the borrower who returns a tool cleaner than they got it vs. the one who returns it at a funeral three years later; "borrow" in permanent quotation marks for the neighbour whose sugar has never once travelled in the other direction.

### 7. Block Parties

- **History (documented):** WWI-era NYC send-off gatherings; WWII growth; 1970s flowering in urban communities, especially the South Bronx, as culture / identity building — the direct descendant, via Harlem house parties, of the rent party (§12).
- **The invite rule is structural:** a block party is for *the block* — everyone on the closed street must be invited, including the neighbour nobody likes and the house that never comes. Leaving a house out "creates awkwardness and hard feelings that last long after the party." NYC's formal version: permit under a tenants'/block association (individuals can't apply alone), majority agreement, free to neighbours, one block, one day, max ~9 hours, streets clear by 9pm. Chicago: alderman-issued, typically free. Many cities require signatures from a supermajority (often cited 75%) of the block — the petition round *is* the onboarding: you knock, introduce, and learn the block before you party on it.
- **Unwritten operating rules:** keep a lane (≈10 ft) for emergency vehicles, hydrants and driveways clear; end on time — the end time you promised the non-partying houses is sacred, because next year's signatures depend on it; planning committee of neighbours, not a solo host; contributions organised so every household — renters, new, long-term — can participate at its level.
- **Social meaning:** a block party is an *audition and a census* in one: who cooks, who watches kids, who has the chairs, who cleans after. Violation comedy: the house that called in a noise complaint on a party it was invited to and didn't attend has chosen a side the block will remember at signature time next year.

### 8. Dominoes / Spades Table Law

**Spades** — the deepest table culture in the sources (see also §3: "If you can't play spades stay far away from the cards table").

| Rule | Phrase / formulation | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| House makes the rules; clarify *before* you sit | "The one steadfast commandment is that the house makes the rules" (Michael Harriot, Deadspin guide). Variants to settle up front: jokers in or out and their order ("Joker, Joker, Deuce"), deuce of diamonds/spades as trump, nil allowed or banned, blind bids, sandbag penalty, target 350 or 500, first hand bids itself (board = 4) | Arguing a rule mid-hand announces you didn't ask — the actual offence |
| **Don't renege** | Reneging = playing off-suit (e.g., cutting with a spade) while holding the led suit. BET's official list: "DON'T RENEGE!... your team loses 3 books." Harriot: a reneger is "the lowest, most despicable form of human cheater who probably brings store-bought potato salad to cookouts and tells people you made it" | Black Card Verified: "getting caught reneging does not just cost you the hand — it costs you credibility at tables you have not even sat at yet. Word travels. The spades community is smaller than it looks and has a longer memory than it should" |
| No table talk — including your face | "No 'ooh,' no sharp inhale, no meaningful pause before you play a card... the first time you violate it, you are not warned — you are just looked at. That look will teach you more about the rule than any explanation could." Tournament rules: table talk = forfeit / −3 books, cheating penalties | The look is the enforcement mechanism for the whole culture in miniature |
| Learn by watching; don't ask at the table | "Nobody sat you down and explained spades... You watched from the doorway when you were too young to play, you got let in as somebody's partner when the odd number needed evening out... asking a basic question at the wrong moment is its own kind of violation"; "The spades table will not be taking questions" | You earn a seat by spectating seasons, not by enthusiasm. Bid is "a conversation and everybody's listening" — overbid = reckless, underbid/sandbag = your partner doing hostile math |
| Don't cut your partner; don't blame your partner at the table | Cutting a book your partner would have won; "You also cannot blame your partner out loud... The post-hand autopsy... happens in the car on the way home, not at the table... why some partnerships are seasonal." Spouses as partners are banned in some houses "for obvious reasons" | Partnerships — and marriages — are the collateral |
| A card laid is a card played; rack your own books | "Make-em, Take-em: If you win a book, it is your responsibility to rack it. If you sit there hesitating over your book, someone will take your shit" | Hesitation is forfeiture |
| Running a Boston is legend | Boston = winning all 13 books (a "dime" = 10); "If you run a Boston, it will be spoken of in your house for generations"; on first hand in some houses it's an automatic game win | The score outlives the night — scores in this culture are *archived orally* |

Scholarly anchor: Amina S. McIntyre, "Reflections on Spades," *American Journal of Play* (2021) — inscribed (informal, customised) vs. printed rules, house rules set by naming trump order, scoring, fair play, dealer; play space as storytelling / "hush harbor."

**Dominoes / "Bones"**

- Bones = the tiles (historically bone/ivory). The slam is language: "When you 'slam' a bone down on the table, it should feel like you're making a statement... bones is a loud game. If you're playing in silence, you're doing it wrong" — momentum and psychological pressure, in Caribbean and many Black U.S. communities a social ritual: "I'm coming through the front door!" on a big double.
- But slam has a ceiling: Jamaican domino teaching video — slamming *too* hard can get you banned; soft play reads as weakness, excessive force as disrespect. Tournament rules split both ways (one Toronto tournament: "Slamming Allowed & Encouraged: But no physical aggression or disrespect"; a Las Vegas championship: *no* slamming / loud talk / profanity — house rules govern, again).
- Knock to pass: if you can't play, rap your knuckles — "It's the sound of defeat." Next player waits for the knock.
- Loser shuffles: "washing the dishes / bust them suds" = shuffling; in team/cutthroat, whoever let the winner out shuffles.
- Scoring talk is part of play: "Give me a Nic" (5), "Dub" (20), "Dub-five" (25), "I'm rolling on twamps" (20) — counting fast "enough not to be annoying," thinking ahead, and trash talk are listed as *skills* alongside scoring.
- **Trash-talk boundary (documented for Jamaican play, generalisable as a table principle):** you may attack skill and intelligence; never family or character. Cross that line and you risk being out of the game *and* the community rotation. Winning is celebrated with boasting — within limits; too much pride and you "play alone next week."
- No table talk in the *signalling* sense (partner hints/gestures = cheating, point penalties/elimination in tournaments) — which coexists with loud *declared* talk. The distinction is the comedy gold: volume is encouraged, information is contraband.

#### 9a. The Candy Lady / Candy House — the kids' economy's institution

- A woman selling from her home/porch: freeze cups / iceberg cups (frozen Kool-Aid in Styrofoam), snowballs/snow cones, pickles (sour, hot, Kool-Aid pickles), penny candy, Now and Laters, Flamin' Hot Cheetos with cheese (and beef if you "really had the money" — "a whole damn meal"). Chicago version: the candy house, supplied historically by a single South Side wholesaler (L&P Foods, 7047 S. State St.).
- Her real function in the sources: "part entrepreneur, part community watch, part summer daydream... a neighborhood historian with a cooler"; "the first person to spot trouble... She noticed who was skipping school, who was hanging with who, and who needed a little extra kindness." Buying candy for other kids was "built on a foundation of Black traditions of acceptance and care," and candy houses were income for Black women in neighbourhoods with half the grocery access — economic infrastructure, not just nostalgia.
- Kid-side etiquette that follows: have your money ready, say thank you, don't bang on the door outside her hours, don't bad-mouth her prices to her face — she is an elder with a business, owed both kinds of respect. (This kid-side list is *inferred* from the institution's description in sources, not quoted from a single list — flagged accordingly.)

### 10. Elder Respect Rules

The most cross-cutting chapter; it governs §§1, 3, 4, 8, 14.

- **"You better speak when you walk into a room."** "Walking into a room and not speaking? That's a surefire way to get called out immediately... A simple 'Good morning' or 'Hey, y'all' isn't just polite; it's a sign of respect. Ignoring people, even unintentionally, is seen as rude and dismissive." Viral grandma formulation: "Baby, when you walk in a room you speak first. That's respect. And I don't raise rude kids. Open your mouth." Southern Black formulation: "If you cross someone['s] threshold open your mouth and speak... Acknowledging your elders [represents] respect. It's a GOLDEN RULE" — failure can mean being told to leave.
- **Elders first, in everything:** "elders come first — no questions asked. Whether it's giving up your seat, letting them get their plate first, or just listening when they speak... Talking back? Not an option. Even a slight side-eye could get you a swift reminder that respect isn't optional." It extends past family: "If an older neighbor needed help with groceries... you listened and followed through." At the cookout: "you approach the elders and speak first, they never come to you. That's a big no-no."
- **Titles, not first names:** elders are Mr./Ms./Auntie/Uncle (fictive kin included — "'Auntie' might just be your mom's best friend"; "Family isn't just blood"). Calling an elder by a bare first name is a scoring-grade violation in every tradition cited.
- **The Look as enforcement:** "The Art of the Side Eye... It can mean anything from 'don't even think about it' to 'I see what you're doing, and I don't approve'... You learn when to stop, when to adjust, and when you've definitely crossed the line." Companion phrases that function as verdicts: "Fix your face." "Don't let me find out." "Act like you got some home training" / "Act like you got some sense" — *home training* is the master concept: public behaviour is read as a report card on your household. The pre-outing speech ("WE ALL HAD THE SPEECH ABOUT GOING IN PUBLIC ACTING LIKE WE AINT HAVE NO HOME TRAINING") is itself a beloved comedy set-piece.
- **The Head Nod (elder/peer protocol):** documented by MEL Magazine, We The Cookout, USC Digital Folklore Archives. Up-nod = someone you know / a peer / at distance; down-nod = respect to an older man or a stranger. Reciprocation matters: not returning a nod is read as hostility or stress (charitable reading: reach out with love, don't lash out). Its weight is highest in predominantly white spaces: "you nod to them... 'how in the hell did I get here' and see another black person nod at you and you go 'Alright cool, at least I'm not alone'"; USC folklore entry: a way to say "I see you," rooted in segregation-era community-finding. Comedy template already in circulation: a creator so offended by an unreturned nod at Whole Foods that he abandoned his salad — mock-protocol tutorial ("glance up and down to check for debts, shared exes, or fade-on-sight; if none, exchange a nod").
- **Kitchen corollary:** "The Kitchen Is Sacred Ground... If Big Mama was working on Sunday dinner, you didn't just wander in uninvited... stay out unless you're helping, and don't touch anything before it's done." Earning a kitchen role is "a sign of trust... once you've perfected a dish? You know you've truly arrived" — same audition system as cookout dishes.

### 11. Minding-Your-Business / "Snitching" Codes — as etiquette, handled neutrally

Present this the way the scholarship does: as a **discretion code with a sliding scale**, rooted in documented distrust, not as a monolith and never as a punchline about crime.

- **The household root is ordinary:** "The Family Business Is the Family's Business — One of the quickest ways to get in trouble in a Black household is spilling family business outside the house... what happens at home stays at home." Barbershop and salon versions (§4) are the adult, public form of the same rule. The porch version: you may *see* everything on the block; repeating it is the violation. Gossip that creates conflict is the breach in community "hood codes" summaries too.
- **The scholarly frame:** Elijah Anderson, *Code of the Street* (1999) — an informal code governing public behaviour in poor inner-city neighbourhoods, centred on **respect** ("juice"), formed, in Anderson's account, as an adaptation to alienation and "a profound lack of faith in police and the criminal justice system." Anderson's own key distinction: residents themselves sort orientations as **"decent"** (mainstream values, hard work, church/school alliances, strict parenting, curfews) and **"street"** — and decent families, while opposing the code's values, "reluctantly encourage their children's familiarity with it for survival." Any treatment that flattens this into "the community's rule" contradicts its primary source.
- **The sliding scale (empirical correction to the slogan):** Clampet-Lundquist et al. (2015), interviews with 77 young men in three Philadelphia neighbourhoods, and Woldoff & Weiss (2010), "Stop Snitchin': Exploring Definitions of the Snitch" — findings as summarised: what counts as "snitching" is *defined situationally* (who was harmed, whether the person was involved, whether victims are neighbours/children); the code of silence is **not unique** to Black neighbourhoods (omertà, "tattletale," blue-wall parallels are cited by the researchers themselves); an *exaggerated* anti-snitching code, the authors argue, weakens the informal social control residents themselves rely on. The 2004 Baltimore "Stop Snitching!" DVD is the documented moment a local slogan went national via media/hip-hop.
- **Etiquette translation for a comedy series (safe ground):** score the *everyday* discretion violations anyone recognises — repeating barbershop talk, telling who was at whose house, narrating a neighbour's argument from your porch like a sportscaster, posting someone's business "for content." Do **not** score witnessing / reporting serious harm; sources themselves treat that as the contested boundary, and making it a gag punches at victims. See Stereotype Risk.

### 12. Rent Parties

- **What they were (documented, Harlem 1920s–30s):** a household facing rent inflated by housing discrimination — Black tenants charged double/triple for the same space — throws a paid party: admission **25¢, later ~50¢**, on **Thursday** (live-in domestics' night off) and **Saturday** (payday, Sunday off). Invitation cards — collected by Langston Hughes — used respectable cover names ("Parlor Social," "Tea Cup Party," "social whist party") and rhyming come-ons ("There'll be plenty to eat and plenty to drink. Don't be bashful — money is what we think").
- **The etiquette was economic and reciprocal:** you *pay at the door without embarrassment and without being chased* — the party is not charity, it's a transaction where the guest gets a night out; you buy food and drink inside (fried chicken, pork chops, pig's feet, potato salad sold from the kitchen; homemade corn liquor — "King Kong"), because plate/drink sales are where the rent actually is; furniture is cleared, rugs rolled back, a stride pianist (Fats Waller, Willie "The Lion" Smith, James P. Johnson all played them; cutting contests to 3am) is the draw — the host's job is a *good party*, the guest's job is to *spend gladly*. Hosting weekly instead of monthly, or fronts run by racketeers/"buffet flats," corrupted the form and were criticised at the time — the community policed the line between mutual aid and exploitation itself.
- **Lineage for the series:** rent party → Harlem house party → Bronx block party (§7) → modern "party with a purpose" / cover-at-the-door house functions. Modern etiquette echo: when a function has a door price for a stated need, haggling the door is the violation; performing charity-pity is a worse one.
- Sources: Frank Byrd's Federal Writers' Project account (1930s, via school archive PDF), Harlem Renaissance histories, rent-party retrospectives — index. **Caution:** Byrd's period account contains era prejudices; cite its facts, not its judgements.

### 13. Moving Day & New Neighbor Onboarding

Documented largely in general etiquette sources (flagged), with block-specific sequencing from §§6–7:

- **Introduce yourself early, before you need anything:** "Introduce yourself in the first two weeks, before you need anything and before anything goes wrong. A neighbor who knows your name will take in your package, tolerate your party and knock on your door rather than call the landlord." Learn names on both sides / across the street; trade a phone number with at least one household.
- **The old neighbours do the first move in the classic script** — a welcome visit / dish (which creates the §6 dish-return obligation) — **but** contemporary apartment polling shows a real split: many residents prefer a hallway "hey" to a door-knock, and read unannounced knocking as intrusion. Score *that tension* (generational/regional, building vs. block) rather than declaring one side correct.
- **Moving day law:** don't block a neighbour's driveway, sidewalk, or car with the truck; don't cut across lawns with furniture ("take the long way"); move in daytime, not dawn/night; clear your debris from sidewalk/street the same day; "leave your old house as clean as you would like to find your new house."
- **Feed your helpers — it is payment, not a bonus:** friends who move you get refreshments through the day and a real meal (pizza is the canonical unit); professional movers get cold drinks, a clear path, packed-and-labelled boxes, you reachable but not hovering, and a tip without fuss.
- **The first-conflict rule (the onboarding payoff):** talk to the neighbour *in person, first, once, politely, assuming they don't know* ("Calling the police or filing a code complaint should only be done as a last resort after you have tried to communicate with your neighbors"; "Do not leave an anonymous note, which always reads as hostile"; "The police are for danger, not for irritation"). A new neighbour whose first contact with the block is a code complaint about a long-standing block norm (a cookout, chairs, music hours) has committed the canonical onboarding violation — they skipped §§1–7 entirely. This is the single most usable new-neighbour rule for satire, and it is documented in a community association's own guidance.

### 14. Church-Adjacent Sunday Rules

- **Sunday morning quiet is a block rule, not just a church rule:** viral formulation: "Down here, you don't mow on Sunday mornings. Sunday mornings is church. That mower stays in the shed until the afternoon at the earliest... what people do Sunday afternoon is 'between you and God'... but during service hours, 'no, you respect your neighbors, you respect what that time means to the people in this community'" — explicitly including non-attendees: you "should still know what time it is and act accordingly." Punchline that carries the rule: "The Lord can hear that mower, and so can everybody else on the street." General etiquette sources converge: no loud equipment Sunday before ~10am; next-to-a-church, not during services at any hour.
- **Saturday-clean / Sunday-best system:** "Saturday mornings in a Black household often started with music blasting and the smell of cleaning products... you relax when the work is done" — Saturday cleaning with music *loud* is licensed (it's the block's shared ritual); Sunday morning is the inverse. "The Church Fits Are Sacred: Sunday mornings meant looking your absolute best. Church clothes were separate from regular clothes... Shoes polished, hair done... You didn't just roll into service; you arrived looking like you respected the occasion."
- **After-church gravity:** Sunday dinner is Big Mama's kitchen law (§10); the cookout / visiting order on Sundays runs *after* service and dinner. Asking a churchgoing elder to do business, favours, or moving help on Sunday morning is a scheduling violation even if they'd never say so.
- Comment threads on the mowing rule split sharply (tradition vs. personal freedom) — again, the argument is inside the community; give both neighbours lines.

### 15. Laundromat Etiquette — *general U.S. sources; not documented as Black-specific*

Present honestly as shared-space law that a block series can use, without claiming a distinct cultural canon:

- **Don't touch other people's laundry** is the headline rule in every guide — with a **timed exception** that is the actual etiquette: wait **5–10 minutes** after a cycle ends (some guides 15–20); then you may move clothes — to a *clean* folding table or their basket, **never the floor**; ideally ask the attendant to do it; never fold a stranger's clothes.
- **Don't be the person who makes that necessary:** be back before your cycle ends; set a timer; if you're not there when it stops and people are waiting, forum consensus since 2005 is blunt: "it's your own damn fault" and the mover is entitled.
- **No reserving / hogging:** a basket on a machine or table doesn't reserve it; don't take four machines for small loads on a busy Sunday; don't spread across three folding tables; take clothes out in a basket, don't fold item-by-item out of a dryer people are waiting for.
- Clean the lint trap, wipe spills, don't use others' supplies, keep kids close (hot machines, chemicals), headphones for media.
- Comedy-native proof the laundromat reads as a neighbourhood institution with status: the Key & Peele "Uncle Ted's laundromat vs. the barbershop across the street" sketch — Ted's pride in his 'mat's "colorful characters," his nephew confessing he's owned a washer/dryer for months, and the barbershop winning by adding a washer/dryer. The joke assumes audiences rank the two institutions against each other.

### 16. Bus Stop Etiquette — *general transit sources; block-relevant applications noted*

- **Boarding order:** let everyone off before you get on; have fare/payment ready (the §2 counter rule, on wheels); move to the back ("Last In, First Out" — don't cluster at the front); pull the cord / press the button **1–2 blocks** before your stop.
- **Seats:** front/priority seats are elders', disabled riders', pregnant riders', parents with small children — and you yield **without being asked**: "Don't wait to be asked: proactively offer your seat; waiting to be asked can be perceived as impolite." This is §10's "give up your seat" rule in transit form, and the one bus rule with a direct elder-respect citation chain. Bags come off the adjacent seat as the bus fills ("Doubled Up" rule); leave a buffer seat while the bus is empty, close ranks as it fills.
- **At the stop itself:** the documented "hood rules" listicle version is *safety* etiquette — track the bus instead of lingering, don't sit in parked cars "kicking it," headphones out / one ear open, face out of your phone, if a stop doesn't look right walk a block up, "make it back home to your FAMILY." **Handle like §2's receipt rule:** this is survival knowledge presented in list form; its comedy in the wild comes from delivery, and a satire series should not strip it to slapstick.
- Volume / space: calls short and low, backpack off your back in a crowd, no speaker media — the barbershop volume rule (§4) applied to transit.

### 17. Comedy / Satire Treatments & Viral Formats — what lands and why

**The mechanism (consistent across every successful example found):** recognition first, punchline second. Audiences do the work — comments are dominated by tagging, "all of the above," adding their own rule #11, and retelling the time *they* did it. Specificity is the credential: Lawry's and garlic salt by name, foil heard before the guest is seen, a chair with duct tape "since 1997." A generic rule ("be polite at parties") never travels; a hyper-specific one ("never that sparkling water mess (read LaCroix)") is a screenshot.

**Recurring formats — describe, don't copy any creator's material:**

1. **Mock-legal codification.** The rule stated as statute/commandment with fake penalties: "Black Barbecue Commandments: Thou shall not make a to-go plate…"; "Man Law: Chapter 6" (reckless eyeballing); a whiteboard "professor" in a lab coat lecturing papi-store rules with a pointer; "Post-Blizzard Etiquet[te]" editorials dating to 1967. *Why it spreads:* the gap between the trivial act and the legal gravity is the joke, and the "penalty" (revoked invitation, lost rotation seat) is social, so everyone can play judge.
2. **The credential at risk — "Black Card Revoked."** Originated as a Cards For All People party game; became a BET game show (2018, hosted by Tony Rock): answer culture questions or lose your "Black Card" and the prize. Street-interview versions quiz strangers in public (mom's pre-store warning = "don't touch nothing"; raisins in potato salad = instant trouble; what itchy ears mean). *Why it spreads:* low stakes, infinite question bank, and the audience answers along a half-second ahead of the contestant. **This is the closest existing analogue to a scoring series — study its question design, not its questions.**
3. **The invite / disinvite referendum.** #WhitePeopleInvitedToTheCookout (2016) and its endless sequels: a public figure does one thing, the timeline votes. Druski's "Cookout Approved White Boy" (30.7M cross-platform views in one ranking) is the sketch version. *Why it spreads:* the verdict is the content; disagreement ("One person's invitation is another person's rejected application") is engagement, not failure.
4. **Direct-to-camera rule lecture while doing something else.** Walking the block (BBQ Etiquette reel: selfie monologue, bottle-in-hand rule, cooler rule), driving (to-go plate commandment), sitting in a salon chair venting about booking policies. *Why it spreads:* the lecture's authority is lived, not staged; commenters litigate individual rules (the BBQ reel's minority calling them "broke people rules" *increased* its reach) — a scoring series should expect and design for rule-by-rule argument in the replies.
5. **"You know you wrong when…" / call-out confession.** Creator confesses or accuses a specific violation (foil out on arrival — 15K likes, 690 mostly-arguing comments; the unreturned head nod that ruined a salad run). Structure: second-person accusation or first-person confession → the comments supply 200 more examples. The polarisation *is* the distribution.
6. **Numbered listicle performance.** "Things Black People Do / Don't Trust / Can't Help Doing" — five numbered acted vignettes, no dialogue needed; commenters reply with numbers ("I'm a 3"). The number-reply is a built-in low-effort engagement mechanic a scored rulebook can borrow structurally.
7. **Two-audience sketch institutions.** Barbershop (the *Barbershop* films; Key & Peele's laundromat-vs-barbershop), cookout, and family-function sketches where one character knows the rules and one is learning them in real time — the learner is the audience's surrogate, and every correction is exposition that doesn't feel like exposition. The "sponsor walk-around" (§3c) is a ready-made version: a host physically walks a newcomer past each elder/station, and each stop is a rule.
8. **Stand-up / podcast "the speech" bits.** The pre-outing home-training speech, the streetlight sprint, the "You got McDonald's money?" exchange ("If you grew up in a Black household, you already know the answer... delivered with a raised eyebrow") — single-phrase bits where the audience finishes the line. These prove a *phrase* can carry a whole rule; a rulebook entry should always lead with its phrase.

**What makes them land, summarised for the series:** (i) the rule is *true first, funny second* — every viral example's top comments validate accuracy before praising humour; (ii) the violator is a *type anyone can be this Saturday*, not an ethnic caricature; (iii) enforcement is a look, a story, or a lost seat — never a lecture about a whole people; (iv) the format invites the audience to add a rule, and the additions are funnier than the post.

## Stereotype Risk / Handle With Care

1. **Laugh at the violation, never at the vernacular.** Every successful source above is insider-voiced. AAVE phrases ("Thou shall not…," "draw back a nub," "home training") are quoted here as *citations*; a series written from outside should have insider writers/performers delivering them, or use the mock-legal register (statutes, commandments, scoring) which is shared American comedy language, rather than imitating dialect for flavour.
2. **Do not flatten regional / house variation into "Black rules."** Spades sources are explicit: the house makes the rules, nil/sandbags/jokers vary, and asking first *is* the etiquette. Arrival-time offsets, kids-vs-elders plate order (sources differ: Uncle Dibbz kids→elders; Vogeg auntie first; Urban Girl elders+children before seconds), and dibs legality all vary. A scoring system that declares one house's rule universal will be corrected — loudly — by the audience it wants. Build the variation into the gag ("Whose house? Then whose rules?").
3. **Survival rules are not folksy rules.** Bag-and-receipt, hands visible while shopping, streetlight-as-safety, bus-stop awareness, and much of Anderson's code arise from policing / surveillance / disinvestment. Sources hold them as wink *and* warning. Using them as light listicle filler, or scoring a *person* for following them, punches in exactly the wrong direction. If the series touches them, the target must be the condition that made the rule necessary.
4. **"Snitching" (§11):** never score reporting harm, never present a monolithic code — the scholarship itself (Anderson's decent/street split; the sliding-scale studies) refutes the monolith. Everyday discretion (§§4/10) is the safe, funny territory.
5. **Poverty is not a costume.** Rent parties, store credit, candy houses, and dibs are mutual-aid under constraint. The comedy in the sources is dignity-forward: hosts deliver a great party, credit is honoured, labour earns respect. Jokes about *having* a tab / a chair / a to-go plate regime are insider affection; jokes about *needing* them are not.
6. **Food rules:** the assigned-dish hierarchy is about trust earned over years, not about any group's cooking ability. The outsider-guest "don't bring a homemade dish" rule is explicitly a *trust-stage* rule in its source — do not generalise it into a claim about anyone's kitchen hygiene beyond the source's own joke, and never aim it at a real ethnic cuisine.
7. **Elders, church, and children are reverence zones in the sources.** The humour around them is affectionate (the ghost in papa's chair, the mower God can hear). Satire that mocks belief, elders' authority *itself* (as opposed to a young violator of it), or kids' safety rules will read as outside contempt, not inside recognition.
8. **The "invite" is not a prize the series can award.** §3c sources are clear the cookout invitation is trust, revocable, and not the series' to confer on real people/groups. Fictional scoring of fictional violators is the safe frame.

## Could Not Verify

- **Borrowing money — amounts, terms, phrases:** no culture-specific documented list found; §6 covers only what store-credit / favour sources support. Do not fill this gap by invention.
- **A distinct Black-urban laundromat or bus canon:** searches returned general U.S. etiquette (cited as such in §§15–16). The institutions are culturally salient (Key & Peele sketch; bus-stop safety lists) but no enumerated insider list was found.
- **Salon rules beyond time/deposit/prep:** stylist-policy sources are commercial policies + two viral rants; deeper salon social etiquette (waiting-room, conversation, children) was thin in results.
- **Block-party unwritten rules beyond invite/end-time/permit:** most sources are municipal how-tos; the richer claims in §7 are drawn from those + one retrospective tutorial, not from an insider etiquette list.
- **"Hood codes" SEO articles** (e.g., Soka World) summarise respect/loyalty/privacy in generic terms with no sourcing — found, read, and *not* relied on for any specific rule above.
- Several social posts are dated 2025–2026 in the index (the research date is 2026-11-08); view/like counts are as reported by the index at read time and will move.

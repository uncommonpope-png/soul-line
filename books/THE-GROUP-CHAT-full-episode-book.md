**A Ledger-Series Book · PLT Press**

---

### The Premise

*The Ledger* proved every conversation has a score. *The Hustle* proved every deal is a conversation. *The Cookout* proved every family is an economy. *The Group Chat* proves the family moved — and took the economy with it.

The group chat is the block's second stoop. It has a government (the admin), a treasury (the money chat), a court (the screenshot department), a press corps (whoever memes first), and one citizen who has said eleven words since last spring and is somehow winning. It has laws. Nobody wrote them down, everybody enforces them, and until now nobody scored them.

Every conversation in this book turns on one named move — the Screenshot Hold, the Dramatic Exit, the 3AM Voice Note Offensive — a play for position in a chat with forty cousins in it. The move is named up front, in plain words. Then the score does what the score always does: it prices the move. Profit. Love. Tax. Power is funny until the Tax comes due.

This book scores the whole life of the chat: the family chat, the money chat, screenshot law, the voice note abusers, the exits and the re-addings, the planning chat where nothing gets planned, the meme economy, the lurker's long silence — and the weekend the chat met in person, after which the admin finally wrote the law down.

### How the Score Works

- **P — Profit.** What a person is gaining: money, position, peace, the last word.
- **L — Love.** Trust, loyalty, standing in the chat — the infrastructure every future favor stands on. It compounds over years of showing up and burns in one forwarded screenshot.
- **T — Tax.** What the chat is costing: time, exposure, obligation, the shame of being seen typing for eleven minutes.
- **PLT** moves as the three move. It rises when Profit and Love rise and Tax falls; it falls the other way. The number is the conversation, told honestly.
- **◆ ratings** run one to five, by final PLT: ◆ under 35 · ◆◆ 35–54.9 · ◆◆◆ 55–69.9 · ◆◆◆◆ 70–84.9 · ◆◆◆◆◆ 85 and above.

### The Moves

Every conversation turns on one named move, stated at the top of the scene with its maxim — one plain sentence, written for this book, in the chat's own language. A move is not good or bad because the admin says so. The score settles it: if a move costs more Tax than the Profit and Love it buys, the number buries it, no matter how strong it looked in the moment. Watch for the moves that win the chat and lose the book.

### The Pink Book — Locked In

In every episode, the pink book — the Betty Plush bunny bookbag — is in frame, same design, set as the series prop, the way it is locked in across *The Ledger*, *The Hustle* and *The Cookout*. In this book it works two shifts: it is the family chat's group picture — the pink book on the table, everybody's hands reaching past it — and it sits physically on the table in every scene where the chat meets in person. It does not drift, it is never renamed. Where the family gathers, in the chat or in the yard, the pink book is already there.

### A Note on the Scripts

This book was written as a script from the start — the book and the episode script are one document, not an adaptation. Each conversation is a performable scene: dialogue-first, five scored beats, each beat one clip. Following each conversation is its episode script: who's in the room, the beats, the on-screen words (lines a character actually says, written to be carried as captions verbatim), the score after, and the takeaway — the book's closing line, verbatim. Production follows the house rules: the dialogue is performed, the scores live on screen, the narration tells the story, and nothing publishes without Ms GCP's approval of the exact post. The **Explainer Line** in each script is a DRAFT sitting in Pope's slot — a candidate written to be replaced by Pope's own word, never presented as it.

All characters are fictional. No real brands, no real people. The chat is every family chat — the conversation is the subject.
